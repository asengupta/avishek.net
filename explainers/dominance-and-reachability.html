Where this shows up in reverse engineering

Dominance and reachability are the first things a tool needs once it has a control-flow graph and wants to say something about it in English, or in another language's syntax, rather than just a list of edges.

  • Narrating an unfamiliar method or paragraph before a human — or a model — tries to explain what it does: finding where a branch's two arms reunite, and exactly which statements belong to a loop, is the structural skeleton any such narration is built on top of. Get the loop body wrong and the narration describes the wrong statements as repeating.
  • Reconstructing structured source from a control-flow graph — the core move inside any decompiler or legacy transpiler — needs a branch's immediate post-dominator before it can emit a single if/else: that is the one statement guaranteed to run after either arm, the natural point to resume straight-line code.
  • A prerequisite, not a substitute, for reducibility: dominance alone describes a region cleanly only once that region is known to have a single entry per loop. An irreducible region needs restructuring first — this page's techniques are what the pages after it build on, not a way around them.

Dominators: looking backward

Pick a statement and ask which earlier statements execution is guaranteed to have already passed through to get there, no matter which way it arrived — the iterative dataflow formulation below is Allen and Cocke's.[1] To reach B3: always through B2, always through B1, always through A — no other way in. Each of those dominates B3. C does not — the other arm reaches B3 without it.

statementdominated by
A{A}
B1{A, B1}
B2{A, B1, B2}
B3{A, B1, B2, B3}
C{A, C}
D{A, D}
E{A, D, E}

Of everything dominating B3, the closest — B2 — is its immediate dominator. Chaining immediate dominators (A → B1 → B2 → B3) is the one unavoidable route in.

Post-dominators: looking forward

Same question, mirrored: which statements is execution guaranteed to reach on the way out, no matter which way it leaves? That is a post-dominator, and it is the answer to the branch question — the first statement both of A's arms are forced through is A's immediate post-dominator.

B1{B1, B2, B3, D, E}

D{D, E}

Notice A has two post-dominators besides itself, D and E — both arms are forced through each of them. D is the immediate one because it comes first: every path from A to E passes through D, so D is closer, and "closest" is the question being asked. Keep that row in mind for what goes wrong.

The slow way: a full set per statement

Give every statement a candidate set, start each one as large as possible, and shrink it to {self} ∪ (intersection of every successor's set), over and over, until nothing shrinks any further. Visited in the same order both times for a fair comparison — D, B3, C, B2, B1, A, each after everything it points to.

Six statements, two rounds, twelve visits — though every set was already final after round 1, because this graph has no cycles and each statement was visited after everything it points to. Round 2, and its whole-collection comparison, only confirms that. The pointer method below needs the same confirming pass on any graph with a loop, so this round is not where the two differ.

The fast way: one pointer per statement

Carry one pointer per statement — its best guess at its immediate post-dominator — instead of a set. This is Cooper, Harvey and Kennedy's algorithm.[2] At each statement, the new guess is the meeting point of its already-visited successors' pointers, found with two "fingers": put one on each successor, then repeatedly advance whichever finger has the lower postorder number to that statement's pointer, until both fingers sit on the same statement. (For post-dominators the postorder is of the reversed graph, walked from the exit; a lower number means farther from the exit.) Like the set version, it repeats passes until one changes nothing.

A chain-walk is bounded by how far apart two pointers' chains are, which in practice is a handful of hops, not the size of the whole method. Where this is used has the numbers from a method large enough for that difference to be the whole story.

Loops: reachability with a stopping point

A different question needs no sets or pointers at all: which statements belong to a loop — the backward walk below, bounded at the header, is Aho, Sethi and Ullman's.[3] Take the loop from visiting a graph. Below, amber marks the statement a step is currently taking off the worklist; a bordered node marks a statement already confirmed as part of the loop body.

flowchart TD
    T --> X1
    T --> L1
    X1 --> X2
    L1 --> L2
    L2 -.->|back edge| T

L2 → T is the back edge, in both of the senses the word gets used: it is the arrow DFS found going to a statement still on its own path, and its target, T, dominates its source, L2 — every path to L2 goes through T. On a reducible graph the two senses always coincide; where they do not, the graph has a loop with more than one entry (see reducibility). A natural loop needs the second sense, and T is this loop's header. The loop's body is everything reachable walking backward from L2, with one rule: stop expanding the moment the walk reaches T, rather than following whatever leads into T from outside the loop.

Loop body: {T, L1, L2}. X1 and X2 are never reached by this walk at all — the other arm out of T, outside the loop — and nothing about them had to be inspected to know that, which is why they never once appear bordered in the diagrams above. The same bounded backward walk, with no stopping point, also answers "what can reach the end" (which decides which statements have a post-dominator at all — see what goes wrong) and, run forward instead of backward, "what is reachable from the start."

What goes wrong

  • Taking any post-dominator for the immediate one. A's strict post-dominators are D and E, and both arms are forced through each. But E is not the closest: every path from A to E passes through D first, so D is the immediate one. A structuring tool that resumed straight-line code at E would put D inside both arms of the if/else.
  • Keeping the whole candidate set instead of a chain of pointers. It is the natural first way to write this down, it gives the right answer, and on a method of a few hundred statements nobody will ever notice it is doing more work than it needs to. The cost only shows up on the methods large enough to matter, which is exactly when nobody is watching closely enough to catch it by inspection — see below.
  • Assuming every statement reaches the exit. Post-dominators are defined by paths to the exit, so a statement that can never reach it — one caught in an infinite loop, say — has no post-dominator at all, and must be left out of the answer rather than defaulted to something. A computation that silently assigns it one anyway will look fine until something tries to walk the resulting structure and finds a cycle or a dangling reference that was never really there. Code unreachable from the start is the mirror problem, for dominators: it has none, though it may still fall through to the exit and so have post-dominators.

Where this is used

cobol-rekt computes forward dominators the whole-candidate-set way, following Allen and Cocke,[1] and picks each statement's immediate dominator by eliminating the candidates that dominate another candidate. Those dominators are what its DJ graph, loop-body detection and reducibility test are built on. It computes no post-dominators.

The post-dominator half of this page comes from a separate Java tool that turns one method's control-flow graph into a nested tree of regions — decisions with two arms and a known end, loops with a known body, everything else a plain sequence — for narrating what the method does, in order. Every decision needs its immediate post-dominator; every loop needs its backward-reachable body.

Most methods in a normal corpus are small enough that either approach — whole candidate sets or a chain of pointers — finishes before anyone would notice the difference. One real method's control-flow graph, though, had on the order of seven thousand statements and eighteen thousand edges — an unusually large single method, the kind a long hand-written dispatcher or a heavily generated chain of conditions produces, but not actually rare in enterprise code once a corpus is big enough. Built the whole-candidate-set way, settling that method's post-dominators alone was doing tens of millions of set comparisons per round, for however many rounds a graph that shape needed — long enough that it looked indistinguishable from a tool that had hung, rather than one that was simply still working. Rebuilt the pointer-chasing way described above, the same method's region tree finished in about a second.

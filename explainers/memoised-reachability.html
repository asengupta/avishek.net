Answering every walk once: strongly connected components and a memo

TL;DRThousands of dynamic jumps ask the same questions about the same programs, including rings of programs that hand control round. Solving each shared part once makes thousands of walks over the same places cost one.

Read first: Value sets and dynamic calls, Visiting a graph, Strongly connected components

A jump such as XCTL PROGRAM(CA-NEXT-PGM) goes to whatever name another program put in the commarea, so the combine walks back through the programs that handed it in. On a large CICS corpus each of about 19,000 jumps walked on its own; one walk reached about 311,000 vertices, and its neighbours walked the same chains again.

PROGB and PROGC both jump to the commarea's name, and both walks go back round one three-program ring to PROGD, which wrote 'PROGE'. Walked one by one they expand 10 places. Keep each place's answer and solve the ring as one strongly connected component, and they expand 6; both get {PROGE}.

Vocabulary: the COBOL/CICS primer. A place is a program, a node (statement) in it and a byte range.

Why each place is solved once

Step 12 of the walk is the saving: C-jump's only successor, B-xctl, was already answered, so the walk took that answer and went no further. That is safe because a place's answer does not depend on who is asking. It is the join of what the place finds itself and the answers of every place it leads to: D-xctl's answer is {PROGE} whether the walk got there from B-jump or from C-jump.

Cycles get in the way of "work out the successors first". In steps 3 to 5 the walk went A-xctl → C-xctl → B-xctl and back to A-xctl: none of them can be finished before the others. But they do not need to be: every place on a cycle reaches every other, so they all reach exactly the same set of places and all have the same answer:

placereaches
A-xctlA-xctl, C-xctl, B-xctl, D-xctl
C-xctlC-xctl, B-xctl, A-xctl, D-xctl
B-xctlB-xctl, A-xctl, C-xctl, D-xctl

A maximal set of places that all reach each other is a strongly connected component; the box in the place graph is one, and every place outside it is a component on its own. Solve the component as one unit: its answer is the join of what each member finds itself, plus the answers of the places the component leads out to. The box has one arrow leaving it, A-xctl → D-xctl.

Tarjan's algorithm finds the components during one depth-first walk, and it finishes each component only after every component it leads to. That is exactly the order the answers need. The walk from B-jump finished the components in this order (steps 9, 10 and 11), and the second walk, from C-jump, finished the last one (step 12):

D-xctlA-xctl B-xctl C-xctlB-jumpC-jump

Each chip comes after every chip its places lead to, so each answer is built from answers already settled. When the walk backs out of a place whose low equals its own index, nothing below it reaches anything older, so that place and everything above it on the stack form one component: pop them, give them their shared answer, and move on. Step 5 is where B-xctl's low dropped to A-xctl's index, 1, and step 10 is where A-xctl, low 1 and index 1, popped the box off the stack.

The textbook version is recursive, one call per place on the current path. Cobble keeps the path in explicit lists instead: the Path row in each step of the walk is that list, four places long at step 4:

The combine had already crashed once on Python's recursion limit when it recursed once per program, and a walk across a corpus can be millions of places deep.

Two smaller changes make each step cheaper. A step that reaches the top of a program asks which links lead into it; it used to scan every link the round knew, and a profiler showed every sample inside that scan. The links are now indexed by what each step looks them up by. For the example's four links, the lookup by callee is the table below: when A-xctl reaches the top of PROGA's linkage (walk step 2), it reads row PROGA and gets its two successors without looking at the other two links.

The strip is B-jump, row 1 of the example's table: program PROGB, node MAIN-PARA/1.then.0 (the XCTL inside the IF), bytes [0, 8) of the linkage section, holding the name PROGD wrote. The combine asks what values the outlined bytes can hold, and answers by walking back from that place. Each step looks at one place, collects any literal values written there, and lists the places the bytes were copied from: a MOVE inside the program, or, at the top of a program's linkage section, the caller that handed the commarea in. From the strip, that is PROGA's transfer; from there, PROGC's and PROGD's.

The walk's answer is everything found at every place it reaches, joined. Done once per jump, as in the naive rows of the table above the walk, the cost is the number of jumps times the number of places each one reaches. Why it matters on a real corpus

The combine, the last stage of cobble's field dataflow, resolves dynamic call targets such as CALL WS-PGM across programs. When the name came in a commarea, the answer is only found by walking back through the program that sent it, and the one that sent it there.

  • CICS programs hand control in rings. In the example, PROGA → PROGB → PROGC → PROGA is such a ring, and the 8-byte commarea goes round with it. Walked backwards, the graph of "where could these bytes have come from" is full of cycles, and every program on a ring sees the same handful of commarea bytes.
  • Many questions, one graph. The example's two jumps walk back over the same ring and reach the same PROGD. On a large CICS corpus each jump started its own walk, and the jumps next to the one in the table below walked the same commarea chains again:

  • The same shape appears whenever many starts ask a reachability question of one fixed graph: which sources can taint this sink, which allocation sites can this variable point to. Read the dashed jumps in the example's graph as sinks and PROGD as a source. Solving the graph once and sharing the answers is the standard way out.

The rule

answer(v) = own(v) ⊔ ⨆ answer(w) for every successor w of v
for a component C: answer(v) = ⨆ own(u) for u ∈ C ⊔ ⨆ answer(w) for edges u → w, u ∈ C, w ∉ C
settle C when Tarjan backs out of a place r with low(r) = index(r): C = the stack down to r
a successor already answered is not entered; each place is expanded once per memo
revisits(v) ⟺ edges out of the places v reaches ≠ (places v reaches) − 1, counting v itself among the places v reaches

Line 1 holds for every member of a component at once, because they all reach the same places; that is why line 2 can give the whole box one answer. Tarjan settles a component only after every component it leads to (the chips in the intuition), so the answers line 2 needs are always there.

The join has to be the walk's join, and it must not care about order or repetition: here, set unions of values, reasons and outside callers, and an "or" of the revisit flag. A naive walk from B-jump meets A-xctl twice in the place graph, once from B-jump and once coming round the box from B-xctl; a union of {PROGE} with {PROGE} is still {PROGE}, and step 10 joins the box's members in any order to the same {PROGE}. That is what lets a stored answer stand in for walking again.

The revisit flag. Besides values, the combine asks whether a walk from a place would reach some place twice: exactly when what it reaches is not a tree, since a tree on k places, counting the start, has k − 1 edges. Line 5, counted on the place graph:

Most places settle the flag when their component settles, without counting. A component of two or more places is a cycle, so every member revisits: row 2, settled in step 10. A single place with an edge to itself, the same successor twice, or a successor that revisits, revisits: rows 3 and 4, B-jump in step 11 and C-jump in step 12, each because its successor revisits. A single place with at most one successor, which does not revisit, does not: row 1, D-xctl in step 9, with no successors at all. What is left is a single place with several successors that do not revisit, like the top of a diamond. The example has none; the shape is this abstract four-place graph, not drawn from the example's programs:

The combine reads the flag in one case only: when a jump's answer has no values and no reasons. A walk that went round a ring and found nothing is reporting that the bytes come from whoever first started the ring, so the target is marked as received from outside (inbound-linkage) rather than left empty with no explanation. In the example, without PROGD, both jumps would end that way: the place graph loses D-xctl, B-jump then reaches 4 places over 4 edges, and finds nothing. What goes wrong

  • The graph must not change while the memo lives. A stored answer is only right for the links it was computed with. Each round of the combine finds new links: a target's values become new edges, which feed other walks (see rounds couple targets in hop limits), so the cross-program memo is built per round and dropped. In the example, round 1 starts with no links: B-jump's walk stops at the top of PROGB's linkage with no values, and C-jump's the same. Round 2 has the four links. Kept into round 2, the round-1 memo would hand B-jump its round-1 answer instead of {PROGE}.
    roundlinks knownplaces answeredB-jump's values
    102: B-jump, C-jumpnone
    246: the place graph{PROGE}
    Inside one program the facts and copies never change, so the second memo, over positions inside a program, lives for the whole run.
  • The memo only shares places that are equal. In the strip below, PROGA's WS-AREA and WS-NEXT-PGM are the same bytes [0, 8): one place, A-xctl. If some copy moved only the first four bytes (the bottom span), the same node reached under [0, 4) would be a second place, expanded on its own, and so would the places behind it, which inherit the narrower range. Copies that move part of a field, and links whose windows line up on different lengths, narrow the range like this, so one node can turn into many places. Telling places apart by bytes rather than field name removed the duplicates that were only names (the top two spans in the strip below); it does nothing for real byte-range fragmentation (the bottom one).

  • One component can be most of the corpus. In the example the box is 3 of the 6 places. On the large corpus the links joined into one cyclic group, and one solve grew past a million places on the stack before anything settled. The memo makes each place cost once; it does not make the set of places small. Hop limits bound that. A hop limit also puts the crossing count into the place, so the same bytes reached after 1 crossing and after 2 are separate places, and the memo shares less.     QS, already reached4P, Q, R, S
    4 edges against 4 − 1: P revisits. That is cheap compared to expanding, but it is not free on a large reach, and its result is cached per start.
  • The memo is mutable. It is the one place in the combine that mutates state, and is excluded from the functional-style lint for that reason: threading the memo and the stack through as values would copy them at every step. In the walk, steps 9 to 12 add their rows (amber) to one Answered table that grows in place, and the Stack row shrinks in place.

Of the two memos, the first answers jumps across programs. Each round builds one over the places it reaches: it resolves a place inside its program and follows each stop across links. A place met again on a cycle is flagged, and an empty answer so flagged becomes inbound-linkage. Links are found through an index by (callee, parameter), (caller, node, parameter) and (callee, node), not by a scan; the links-into table is the first of those for the example. The second memo works inside one program and lasts the whole run. Both tell places apart by their bytes alone, not by the field name that reached them.

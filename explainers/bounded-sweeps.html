Sweeps: a fixpoint with a budget that says when it ran out

TL;DROn a large corpus, a few tangled programs can take most of the run. This puts a known budget on each program and marks every answer it cut short, so you get results on time plus a list of call sites to check by hand — never a silently smaller answer.

Read first: Visiting a graph, Fixpoints and worklists

A whole-corpus fixpoint is as slow as its slowest program: on a corpus of about 3,800 programs, an earlier version of the walk spent 1,404 s, 2,324 s and 4,434 s on the last three programs. A worklist cannot say how far along a program is, and capping it is only safe if a capped program says so instead of quietly returning less.

PROGA's loop writes WS-NEXT; a LINK after the loop reads it. A budget of 10 passes lets the walk go round the program at most 10 times; it finds both targets and settles on pass 2. Stopped after one pass, the LINK gets only PROGB, marked incomplete-walk.

The walk is the worklist from fixpoints and worklists: a queue of nodes whose entry state changed, taken in reverse postorder. A pass (cobble's flag and log lines call it a sweep) is one trip of the walk's place in that order, its cursor, from the start of the order to the end.

Besides one node per statement, the graph has a label at the start of each paragraph, a separate node for the loop's test, and for each EXEC CICS a #raise node (did the command raise a handled condition?) and a #dispatch node (where a handler would take it). The test's FALSE arm goes to the PERFORM (MAIN-PARA/0); its TRUE arm goes to the LINK (MAIN-PARA/1). The graph also has a PERFORM edge into PICK-PARA and a RETURN edge out of its end, but the walk follows neither. It walks PICK-PARA on its own, a range walk, from the state the PERFORM is visited with, and hands what leaves PICK-PARA#end to the PERFORM's resume, the node control comes back to: here the test (the thick edge). A RETURN edge leads to every caller of the paragraph at once; the resume belongs to one call.

The reverse postorder cobble computes for it — the numbers in the graph — from depth-first search over all of these edges, taking the test's FALSE arm first:

MAIN-PARA#labelMAIN-PARA/0#loop#testMAIN-PARA/1MAIN-PARA/1#raiseMAIN-PARA/2MAIN-PARA/2#raiseMAIN-PARA/2#dispatchMAIN-PARA/1#dispatchMAIN-PARA/0PICK-PARA#labelPICK-PARA/0PICK-PARA/1PICK-PARA#end

In the chips, the code after the loop (chips 3 to 8) comes ahead of the PERFORM (chip 9). The loop on the traversal page put the body first, because its search took the body's arm first. Either way the test, chip 2, comes before both, and the PERFORM resumes at it, behind the cursor: that is what decides how many passes the loop needs. Only the nine MAIN-PARA nodes are ever on the program's own queue; chips 10 to 13 are visited inside the range walks.

The tables in the walk below show, for each node in the graph, the values WS-NEXT can hold on entry. PROGB is the VALUE clause; PROGC is written by PICK-PARA/0, node 11. WS-I is tracked too, and "settled" needs its states to stop changing as well. ADD 1 TO WS-I (node 12) is not a literal, so it is not evaluated: the write gives WS-I no values, only the reason not-a-literal (see the reasons taxonomy). Joined with the VALUE at the test, that is {00} plus not-a-literal after the first trip round the loop, and it does not grow after that; the walk does not count the loop to 4.

The budget changes only where the walk may stop, and how far along it is becomes a fraction of a known whole — the "sweep 2 of 10" in the log lines under why a pass budget can stop early, rather than the queue lengths in the table under why not the worklist's budget.

Why a pass budget can stop early

One pass moves the cursor once along reverse postorder, taking each queued node it comes to. At each node it applies the statement to the node's entry state and pushes the result into each successor's entry state, joining it with whatever is there; a successor whose state grew is queued. A node that is not queued is skipped: its entry state has not changed, so visiting it would change nothing. When nothing queued lies ahead of the cursor, the pass ends and the cursor wraps to the earliest queued node.

A loop needs a second pass. Passes 1 and 2 of the walk, cut down to the nodes of the loop that matter, in the order the program's walk takes them:

dequeue in the passnodepass 1pass 2
1st (2nd in pass 1)the testpushes {PROGB}pushes {PROGB, PROGC}
nextthe LINK, after the loopsees {PROGB}sees {PROGB, PROGC}
lastthe PERFORM: its range walk's MOVE 'PROGC'writes PROGCwrites PROGC
lastthe PERFORM: its resumehands {PROGC} back to the testhands back the same

The test comes before the body in reverse postorder, because the test is how the body is reached. In pass 1 the LINK in row 2 sees only what was true before the loop ran: the write in row 3 reaches the test only at row 4, through the PERFORM's resume, after the test has had its turn. In pass 2 the test starts with the body's write in its state and hands it on. A value has to go back round the loop once before it reaches the code after the loop. That is why two passes is the useful floor.

A forward chain is carried in one pass, however long. Reverse postorder puts every node after all the nodes that lead into it, except along back edges. Take PROGX, a chain of copies through two PERFORMs (its four fields are PIC X(4)):

           PERFORM P1.

           MOVE WS-A TO WS-B.
           PERFORM P2.

Every definer sits earlier in the chips row than its read, and the walk takes them in that order: P1 is walked when MAIN-PARA/1 is taken, P2 inside that walk when P1/1 is, and MAIN-PARA/2 after both have returned. So each write has been pushed forward by the time its read is visited. This graph has no back edge, and the queue empties in the first pass — its log line is PROGX sweep 1 of 10 done, 5 nodes reached, settled. The 5 nodes are the MAIN-PARA nodes on the program's own queue; P1's and P2's are visited inside range walks and not counted there.

An empty queue proves the fixpoint. When nothing is queued, every node's entry state already holds what its predecessors last sent, so another visit anywhere would change nothing. That is the end of pass 2 of the walk. The member is settled: the walk ran to the end, and its answer is exactly the unbounded walk's, because it is the same walk. If the budget runs out with nodes still queued, it is unsettled. Cobble logs which one each pass was:

PROGA sweep 1 of 10 done, 9 nodes reached, still growing
PROGA sweep 2 of 10 done, 9 nodes reached, settled

No pass is spent only on proof: the pass that carries PROGC to the LINK is the one whose queue empties. The 9 nodes are the MAIN-PARA nodes, as in the chain.

Unsettled means missing, never wrong. Every transfer and the join are monotone (see the rule), so a state stopped early is a subset of the fixpoint's. Pass 1's table against the settled one:

WS-NEXT on entry toafter 1 passfixpoint
MAIN-PARA/1 (LINK){PROGB}{PROGB, PROGC}
PICK-PARA/0{PROGB}{PROGB, PROGC}
PICK-PARA#end{PROGC}{PROGC}

Every value in the middle column is also in the right-hand one, so every flow read off an early state is real; what may be absent is a value that needed more passes, like PROGC in row 1. Cobble says so: every dynamic call target and every jump in an unsettled member carries the reason incomplete-walk, as in pass 1's target row (see the reasons taxonomy for the full list of reason codes), and the run's summary counts and names the member as unsettled. A walk in the combine that passes through that member's jump facts carries incomplete-walk on into the targets it feeds in other programs.

Why not the worklist's budget

Without --sweeps, the walk's budget is --iteration-factor: that many dequeues per CFG node (200 by default), counting the range walks' dequeues, after which it publishes what it has, unsettled. The example's graph has 14 nodes (MAIN-PARA#end unreachable): a cap of 200 × 14 = 2,800 dequeues; it finishes in 24. On the large corpus the worklist finished every program, but the run spent over an hour waiting on one.

A worklist is also hard to watch. Its queue on the example, after each of the program's own 17 dequeues, as cobble runs it; the range walks run inside dequeues 9 and 17 and take 4 and 3 more:

dequeuesnodes takenqueue length after each
1–9all 9 MAIN-PARA nodes, MAIN-PARA#label to MAIN-PARA/01 2 2 3 3 3 2 1 1
10–178 again, MAIN-PARA/0#loop#test to MAIN-PARA/02 2 3 3 3 2 1 0

Nothing in it counts down. After dequeue 9 the queue holds one node, exactly as after dequeue 8, and yet 8 more follow; after dequeue 16 it holds one again, and only one follows. A progress line can only estimate from how fast the queue drains. A budget of passes is known before the walk starts, still gives the exact answer when it has time to, and marks its results, like the LINK's row in pass 1, when it does not:

walkcap, known before it startsused
--iteration-factor 200200 × 14 = 2,800 dequeues24
--sweeps 1010 passes2 passes (24 dequeues)

Both caps are known up front, but only the second says how far along the walk is: after pass 1 it is one pass into at most 10, and its log line says sweep 1 of 10.

Where this shows up

When the goal is one answer — which programs each CALL WS-PGM or EXEC CICS XCTL/LINK PROGRAM(WS-...) can reach — a few tangled programs should not hold the rest back, nor quietly return less than they found.

  • Dynamic call targets across a large CICS corpus. On the corpus of about 3,800 programs, the per-program fixpoint decided how long the pipeline took. Most programs settled in seconds. A handful took most of the run.
  • A budget a reviewer can read. A call target that comes back with incomplete-walk means "these programs at least, maybe more": a list of call sites to check by hand, not a silent gap. Cobble's targets.jsonl rows for the example's LINK, at one pass and at two:
--sweeps 1:
{"caller": "PROGA", "node": "MAIN-PARA/1", "kind": "program",
 "transfer": "CICS_LINK", "expression": "WS-NEXT",
 "candidates": ["PROGB"],
 "reasons": ["incomplete-walk"], "dropped": []}
--sweeps 2:
{"caller": "PROGA", "node": "MAIN-PARA/1", "kind": "program",
 "transfer": "CICS_LINK", "expression": "WS-NEXT",
 "candidates": ["PROGB", "PROGC"],
 "reasons": [], "dropped": []}

The first row's "reasons": ["incomplete-walk"] is what tells a reviewer that "candidates": ["PROGB"] may be short, and the second row adds PROGC. Any whole-program analysis run under a time limit makes the same trade: let every program finish, or cap the work and mark what the cap touched. The marking is what makes the cap safe to use.

The rule

in(n) is node n's entry state; transfer_n applies n's statement to a state; ⊔ is the join, which keeps every value either side holds; N is the --sweeps budget.

order = reverse postorder of the nodes the entry reaches; queue = {entry}
one step: take the first queued n at or after the cursor, else wrap to the earliest;
          after = transfer_n(in(n)); for each successor s: in(s) = in(s) ⊔ after, queue s if it grew
a PERFORM: walk its range from after, by this same rule and budget;
          in(resume) = in(resume) ⊔ what leaves the range's end; RETURN edges are never followed
a pass ends at each wrap; stop when the queue is empty, or when pass N + 1 would start
settled = this walk's queue and every range walk's queue emptied
unsettled ⇒ in(n) ⊆ fixpoint in(n) for every n; every target and jump gets incomplete-walk

Each line is a step of the walk above. order is the 13-node chips row. The PERFORM line is dequeue 9, whose range walk hands {PROGC} back to the test and grows its {PROGB}. The wrap is the start of pass 2, and the empty queue is its end. The unsettled ⇒ line is the budget-1 chips with incomplete-walk.

A value written in a loop body reaches a reader after the loop on pass 2, as the LINK's row in the walk does. Each further step it has to take backward against the order — round the loop again, or to a copy earlier in the same body, as in the second item below — costs one more pass. What goes wrong

  • Found is not settled. A budget can stop the walk with every candidate already in the LINK's entry state and nodes still queued. The run is unsettled, and the mark stays on a complete answer. In the PERFORM-twice program below:
    walkLINK candidatesreasons
    --sweeps 1PROGCincomplete-walk
    --sweeps 2PROGCnone
    The candidates are identical, and row 1 still carries incomplete-walk: the walk cannot know a state is final until its queue is empty.
  • Some loops need more. If the body copies the other way round, the value has to go round the loop twice to reach the LINK. In this variant WS-CODE has a VALUE of its own: 
               05  WS-CODE          PIC X(8) VALUE 'PROGD'.
      ...
       PICK-PARA.
           MOVE WS-CODE TO WS-NEXT.
           MOVE 'PROGC' TO WS-CODE.
           ADD 1 TO WS-I.
    budgetLINK candidatessettled
    --sweeps 1PROGBno
    --sweeps 2PROGB, PROGDno
    --sweeps 3PROGB, PROGC, PROGDyes
    --sweeps 4PROGB, PROGC, PROGDyes
    In pass 1 the range walk copies WS-CODE's PROGD into WS-NEXT and only then writes PROGC into WS-CODE; both go back to the test through the PERFORM's resume. Pass 2 carries PROGD to the LINK, and its range walk copies the PROGC now in WS-CODE into WS-NEXT, which goes back to the test a second time. Pass 3 carries it to the LINK; its range walk changes nothing at the range's end, and the queue empties. The LINK gains one candidate on pass 2 and another on pass 3, and the walk settles in pass 3.
  • Back edges without a loop. Reverse postorder is computed over every edge in the graph, PERFORM and RETURN edges included. A paragraph PERFORMed from two places has RETURN edges to both, so the second PERFORM can be reached through the paragraph's end and comes after it in the order, and its edge back into the paragraph is a back edge. This program has no loop in its source: 
           MAIN-PARA.
           PERFORM SET-PARA.
           PERFORM SET-PARA.
           EXEC CICS LINK PROGRAM(WS-NEXT) END-EXEC.
           EXEC CICS RETURN END-EXEC.
       SET-PARA.
           MOVE 'PROGC' TO WS-NEXT.
    flowchart TD
    A["MAIN-PARA/0 first PERFORM · 2"] -->|PERFORM| S["SET-PARA#35;label · 3"]
    S --> W["SET-PARA/0 MOVE 'PROGC' · 4"]
    W --> E["SET-PARA#35;end · 5"]
    E -->|RETURN| B["MAIN-PARA/1 second PERFORM · 12"]
    E -->|RETURN| K["MAIN-PARA/2 LINK · 6"]
    B -.->|"PERFORM · back edge"| S
    A ==>|resume| B
    B ==>|resume| K
    linkStyle 5 stroke:#f5b041,stroke-width:3px
    The graph shows the nodes around SET-PARA, leaving out the entry and the #raise and #dispatch nodes; the numbers are places in reverse postorder. The second PERFORM is last, 12th, and its resume, the LINK, is 6th. The walk follows the thick resume edges, not the RETURNs. It takes the first PERFORM, walks SET-PARA from {PROGB}, and hands {PROGC} to the second; that one walks SET-PARA again from {PROGC} and hands {PROGC} to the LINK. The LINK lies behind the cursor, so visiting it starts pass 2. With --sweeps 1 the LINK's entry already holds {PROGC}, the whole answer, but it is still queued; --sweeps 2 visits it and the chain after it, and the queue empties.
  • The budget binds range walks too. Each range walk counts its own passes over its own order and stops at the same budget. A member is settled only if its own walk and every range walk it ran emptied their queues. Move the example's loop into a paragraph of its own, OUTER-PARA. PERFORM PICK-PARA UNTIL WS-I > 3., and PERFORM OUTER-PARA before the LINK: the program's own walk has no loop and empties its queue in pass 1, but with --sweeps 1 the walk of OUTER-PARA stops with its test still queued, and the member comes out NOT SETTLED after 1 sweeps, its LINK at PROGB with incomplete-walk. With --sweeps 2 it is settled in 1 sweeps — the count is the program's own walk's passes — with PROGB and PROGC.
  • The mark is per program, not per site. An unsettled program marks every dynamic target and every jump with incomplete-walk, including ones whose values could not change any more. The walk does not track which sites were still growing. Put a second LINK ahead of the loop, EXEC CICS LINK PROGRAM(WS-FIRST) with WS-FIRST given VALUE 'PROGD' and never written, and run it with --sweeps 1:
    sitecandidatesreasons
    LINK PROGRAM(WS-FIRST)PROGDincomplete-walk
    LINK PROGRAM(WS-NEXT)PROGBincomplete-walk
    Row 1 has its whole answer and nothing in the loop can change it, but it carries the mark because the member as a whole did not settle in one pass. With --sweeps 2 both rows lose it, and row 2 gains PROGC.
  • Missing is still missing. No value in an unsettled result is invented, but a missing candidate is a missing edge in the call graph. Read the --sweeps 1 row under where this shows up without its reasons and the call graph is: 
    flowchart LR
    A["PROGA"] -->|LINK| B["PROGB"]
    A -.->|"missing at --sweeps 1"| C["PROGC"]
    The dashed edge is the one the settled walk finds. The incomplete-walk reason on the row is the only thing that says it may be there. A consumer that reads candidates and ignores reasons will treat a partial answer as complete.
  • The budget bounds passes, not work. A pass takes each queued node of the program's own walk at most once, but every PERFORM it takes can run a range walk of up to N passes of its own, and ranges perform other ranges. So N does not fix the number of dequeues: pass 1 of the example took 13, 4 of them in a range walk. What the budget fixes is how many times each walk may go round, and a settled run costs exactly what the unbounded walk costs.

In cobble

The fixpoint (cobble-dataflow-fixpoint) runs the worklist capped at --iteration-factor × nodes dequeues by default (factor 200) — the 2,800 for the example under why not the worklist's budget. --sweeps N bounds the same walk by N passes instead, in every PERFORM range walk as well as the program's own. The two flags cannot be combined, and --sweeps 0 is rejected:

cobble-dataflow-fixpoint: error: argument --iteration-factor: not allowed with argument --sweeps
cobble-dataflow-fixpoint: error: argument --sweeps: needs at least one sweep, got 0

A member with no back edge empties its queue in one pass, as the chain program's log line in why a pass budget can stop early shows. For each member the log says settled in N sweeps or NOT SETTLED after N sweeps; the closing warning names every unsettled member, and the summary counts them under unsettled. For the example with --sweeps 1:

PROGA 2/2 writes covered, 1 unhandled, 0 unmodelled, 3 edges, 2 of 2 extents tracked, 0.0s, NOT SETTLED after 1 sweeps -- edges are a subset
...
!! 1 members still growing when their walk's budget ran out (PROGA); their edges are a subset of the flows those programs have
{"analysed": 1, ..., "unsettled": 1, ...}

A walk that stops before settling adds the incomplete-walk reason to the targets and jumps it found, as in the targets.jsonl rows under where this shows up. The flags are documented in the field dataflow reference. Next: answering every walk once.
